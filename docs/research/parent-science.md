# The evidence behind the Parent Guide

Confidence labels used below: **[STRONG]** replicated, large samples ·
**[MODERATE]** consistent but limited · **[WEAK]** suggestive or confounded ·
**[CONTESTED]** failed replication or active dispute.

A caution that applies throughout: most intervention research (breathing,
reappraisal, expressive writing, power posing) was done on **adults or
adolescents**, not on 6-to-10-year-olds. That is flagged each time it matters.

---

## 1. Does practice actually move scores?

**[STRONG] Scharfen & Holling (2018), *Intelligence*** — meta-analysis of retest
effects: 122 studies, N = 153,185.

| Sitting | Effect |
|---|---|
| Test 1 to 2 | **d = 0.27** (about 4 IQ points) |
| Test 2 to 3 | d = 0.15 |
| Test 3 to 4 | d = 0.10 |

Gains are **front-loaded and shrink fast**. Identical forms produce larger gains
than parallel forms. **Children show larger retest effects than adults.**
<https://gwern.net/doc/iq/2018-scharfen.pdf>

**[MODERATE] Kulik, Kulik & Bangert (1984), *AERJ*** — on intelligence tests,
practice alone is about **0.25 SD**; practice **plus coaching** about **0.51 SD**.
<https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00028312021002435>

**[MODERATE] Bangert-Drowns, Kulik & Kulik (1983), *RER*** — effects were
**smallest for brief test-taking orientation** and **largest for extended
drill-and-practice**. <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543053004571>

**This is the most important pair of numbers in the guide.** The brief-orientation
end — knowing the format, following directions, marking answers — is where nearly
all of the defensible benefit sits, **and it is the smallest effect**. The large
effects come from exactly the intensive drilling that creates the problems below.

**[MODERATE] te Nijenhuis et al. (2007)** — score gains from practice and coaching
are largely **not** gains in general ability; they are test-specific and "hollow"
with respect to *g*. <https://gwern.net/doc/iq/2007-tenijenhuis.pdf>

### What the CogAT publisher itself says

Riverside Insights, *Cognitively Speaking: Using Ability Tests in Gifted and
Talented Identification Programs* — unusually candid:

- Unequal preparation arises both by accident and from **well-meaning parents who
  obtain practice materials**.
- Prior practice correlates with **higher socioeconomic status**, producing unfair
  outcomes and **reduced diversity** among selected students.
- Their remedy is **not to ban practice but to equalise it**: schools should give
  *all* students the free official practice materials.
- Adequate practice must go **beyond reading the directions** — students should
  attempt real items with feedback.

<https://info.riversideinsights.com/hubfs/Cog%20Speakings/Cognitively%20Speaking%20-%20Using%20Ability%20Tests%20in%20Gifted%20and%20Talented%20Identification%20Programs.pdf>

The ethical line falls out of this naturally. The publisher's concern is not that
a child is *prepared*, but that preparation is **unequally distributed**.

### What NAGC says — and what it does not

NAGC's positions: identification should combine objective and subjective
measures; **a single test at a single point in time should not decide**;
identification should happen over time with multiple opportunities.
<https://www.nagc.org/identification>

Reported honestly: across NAGC's position statements I found **no statement
specifically addressing parent-driven test preparation**. NAGC's relevant
contribution is structural — *don't let one score decide* — which is itself the
best argument for a parent to stop squeezing points out of one sitting.

### The score is less stable than the label suggests

**[STRONG] Lohman & Korb (2006), *JEG* 29(4)** — 6,321 students, ITBS, grades 3-8:

- Only about **40%** of children in the top 3% in grade 3 were still there in
  grade 4.
- By grade 8, only **35-40%** of the original group remained.
- This happened **despite** a highly reliable test (KR-20 = .98) that was stable
  year to year (r = .91).

<https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ746292.pdf>

> Riverside's own document summarises this as "just 60%" retention. The primary
> source says **about 40%**. Cite the primary source.

### The over-placement argument, stated carefully

1. Coaching gains are largely test-specific, not gains in reasoning.
2. So a coached score overstates the ability the program will demand daily.
3. Independently, even an honestly earned extreme score regresses substantially.
4. Coaching adds systematic inflation on top of that random regression.

This is **reasoning from converging evidence, not a single study**. I found no
study that tracked coached-in gifted placements to later outcomes. That specific
claim should not be asserted as demonstrated.

### The familiarisation / coaching line

| Familiarisation — defensible, low yield | Coaching — large effects, the real problem |
|---|---|
| Seeing each format once so nothing is novel | Repeated drilling of many items per format |
| Practising *how to respond* — clicking, not skipping | Teaching rule-sets for solving matrix items |
| Practising listening to spoken directions | Memorising item types or leaked items |
| One short session with official materials | Multi-week commercial prep courses |
| Explaining that some questions will be too hard | Training the child to a target score |

## 2. Test anxiety

### Mechanism

**[STRONG] Arnsten (2009), *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*** — the prefrontal
cortex is the brain region **most sensitive** to stress; even mild uncontrollable
stress causes rapid loss of prefrontal function including working memory.
<https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2648>

**[STRONG] Ashcraft & Krause (2007)** — anxiety works like a **concurrent second
task**: worry consumes the same working memory the problem needs. PMID 17694908

**[STRONG] Beilock & Carr (2005), *Psychological Science*** — under pressure,
**only individuals high in working memory capacity were harmed**. PMID 15686575

**[STRONG] — and the key study for this audience — Ramirez, Chang, Maloney,
Levine & Beilock (2016), *JECP*** — in **early elementary school**, children with
the **highest working memory** avoided advanced strategies when math-anxious and
underperformed relative to lower-working-memory peers. PMID 26342473

> **The sentence the whole guide rests on:** the children most likely to be
> identified as gifted are the children most likely to lose ground under
> pressure. Calm is not a nicety for a bright anxious child; it is the condition
> under which their advantage is visible at all.

**[MODERATE] An honest counterweight. Akan, Yildirim & Wolf (2025), *J
Intelligence*** — with verified cortisol elevation, **neither acute stress nor
test anxiety significantly affected IQ test scores** in adults. PMID 41149770

Defensible synthesis: anxiety reliably taxes working memory *during* demanding
tasks, but one stressful morning is not reliably enough to sink a whole ability
test. Aim for calm without believing one nervous child equals one ruined score.

### The Yerkes-Dodson curve — **[CONTESTED]**

The 1908 original used **12 Japanese dancing mice**, never measured arousal,
ran roughly 2-4 mice per condition, performed **no statistical tests**, and found
the inverted-U in **one of three experiments**. Replications in chicks and kittens
failed. Modern human studies mostly show a monotonic pattern: more stress, worse
performance.

<https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/2/29-1> ·
<https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613(24)00078-0>

Fine as a **visual metaphor**. Not a law. And note the practical asymmetry: for a
difficult reasoning task in a young child, **lower arousal is better**. There is
no optimal stress level a parent should be trying to induce.

### Prevalence

**[STRONG] Robson, Johnstone, Putwain & Howard (2023), *J School Psych*** — 76
studies, **53,617 children aged 5-12**:

- Test anxiety is **measurable from about age 7**.
- Mathematics achievement r = -0.21; literacy r = -0.20; academic self-concept
  r = -0.41.
- Girls report more than boys (d = 0.21), and the gap is **smaller in grades 1-2
  (r = 0.14) than grades 3-4 (r = 0.28)**.

<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37253582/>

> **No defensible single prevalence percentage exists for grades 1-4.** The
> meta-analysis reports correlations, not prevalence. The guide prints no
> percentage.

**[STRONG] von der Embse et al. (2018)**, 238 studies — **perceived test
difficulty and high-stakes consequences** are associated with elevated test
anxiety, and **self-esteem was the strongest predictor**. PMID 29156362

### How parent anxiety transmits — the single best study

**[STRONG] Maloney, Ramirez, Gunderson, Levine & Beilock (2015), *Psychological
Science* 26(9)** — first and second graders, 438 analysed:

- When parents were more math anxious, children learned **significantly less
  math** across the year and ended with more math anxiety — **but only when those
  parents reported helping frequently with homework**.
- When math-anxious parents helped **less** often, there was **no relation**.
  Interaction F(1, 430) = 4.59, p = .033.
- Domain-specific: no equivalent effect on reading.
- Survived controls for parents' math knowledge and school SES. The transmission
  is **affective, not informational**.

<https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797615592630>

> **This is the most actionable finding in the whole report, and the most
> important warning for an app like this one.** The damage is not caused by an
> anxious parent existing. It is caused by an anxious parent **doing repeated
> academic drilling with the child**.

## 3. Calming techniques

Overarching caveat: almost all pediatric RCT evidence comes from **medical and
dental procedures**, not testing rooms.

| Technique | Confidence | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Belly breathing** | **[MODERATE] — best supported** | Reviewed across 13 studies, ages 6-18 (Tsakona et al. 2025, PMID 39857890). A visual feedback object improved results in a 7-11 RCT (Yendodu & Nirmala 2025). |
| **Box breathing** | **[WEAK]** | No distinct pediatric evidence. The 4-second **hold** is uncomfortable for many young children. Prefer simple long-exhale breathing at this age. |
| **5-4-3-2-1 grounding** | **[WEAK]** | A search returning 548 grounding results produced **no empirical support** for this specific technique in children or adults. Clinically derived, plausible, risk-free. Say so. |
| **Progressive muscle relaxation** | **[MODERATE]** | RCT ages 7-11, p < 0.001 (Tanna et al. 2025, PMID 40169513); ages 8-17, d = 1.12 (Bucur et al. 2025, PMID 40428834). |
| **Power posing** | **[CONTESTED] — do not present as evidence-based** | Hormonal and performance claims **do not replicate** (Ranehill 2015; Barel 2024 PMID 39633430; Metzler & Grèzes 2019 PMID 31245173; Mikkelsen 2025 PMID 40534239). A preparatory pose did **not** improve oral exam performance (PMID 37377508). Only weak self-report effects survive. |
| **Arousal reappraisal** ("I feel excited") | **[MODERATE] in adults, [WEAK] in children** | Jamieson et al. 2010 (PMID 20161454); Brooks 2014 (PMID 24364682). **No trial in 6-10 year olds.** Cheap and benign; bill as promising, not proven. |
| **Expressive writing before a test** | **[CONTESTED] — do not recommend here** | Ramirez & Beilock (2011, *Science*) **failed a preregistered high-powered replication** (Camerer et al. 2018, *Nature Human Behaviour*). It was also tested on 9th graders and college students. |
| **Branded mindfulness programs** | **[CONTESTED] for young children** | Meta-analysis found no significant effect (PMID 40407584). The INSPIRE cluster RCT found **relaxation reduced depression in primary schools while mindfulness *increased* depressive symptoms** there (PMID 42584489). Prefer simple relaxation. |
| **Exercise** | **[MODERATE], but note the dose** | 20 min aerobic, 2-3x per week, for **at least 4 weeks** reduced test anxiety. **Single 10-15 min sessions showed no significant reduction** (Zhang et al. 2022, PMID 35682293). Belongs in the week plan, not the morning checklist. |

## 4. Sleep

**[STRONG] Paruthi et al. (2016)**, AASM consensus, endorsed by the AAP:

| Age | Hours per 24h, including naps |
|---|---|
| 3-5 years | 10-13 |
| **6-12 years** | **9-12** |
| 13-18 years | 8-10 |

<https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.5866>

**[STRONG] Gruber, Cassoff, Frenette, Wiebe & Carrier (2012), *Pediatrics*** —
34 typically developing children aged 7-11, **teachers blinded to condition**. A
**cumulative extension of about 27 minutes** produced detectable improvement in
emotional lability and restlessness; a cumulative restriction of about 54 minutes
did the reverse. PMID 23071214

**[STRONG] Sadeh, Gruber & Raviv (2002)** — children with **fragmented** sleep
performed worse specifically on **complex** tasks. That is the profile of a
reasoning test. PMID 11949899

> **This is the strongest evidence in the whole report for a 7-day framing rather
> than a night-before framing.** Cumulative differences under an hour, accrued
> over several nights, are teacher-visible in a 7-to-11-year-old.

> **Honesty flag:** the widely repeated claim that *"the sleep two nights before
> matters more than the night before"* could **not be verified** in the pediatric
> literature. The guide does not print it. The defensible and more reassuring
> version: what accumulates across the week matters more than any single night.

**[MODERATE] Hale & Guan (2015), *Sleep Medicine Reviews*** — 67 studies; screen
time adversely associated with sleep in **90%** of them, mainly shorter duration
and later timing. The authors are explicit that **causation is not confirmed**.
PMID 25193149. A later study in 6-11 year olds found **bedtime consistency
mediated** the link — consistency may be the fixable variable. PMID 42142028

## 5. Food and drink

**[MODERATE] for eating breakfast; [WEAK] for what is in it.**

**Adolphus, Lawton, Champ & Dye (2016), *Advances in Nutrition***, 45 studies:
breakfast versus fasting gives a short-term, same-morning benefit to **attention,
executive function and memory**, clearest in undernourished children. Critically,
**firm conclusions cannot be drawn about breakfast composition**. PMID 27184287

> **Correction to a common app claim.** "A high-protein low-glycemic-index
> breakfast improves test scores" is **not well supported**. Only "eating
> breakfast beats fasting" is. A stable-glucose breakfast is a reasonable
> low-risk bet, not a proven intervention.

**[WEAK] Hydration.** Edmonds & Burford (2009), 58 children aged 7-9: extra water
improved visual attention (p = 0.019) and a memory task (p = 0.014). PMID 19501780.
But a 2026 study of 200 children found **no association** (PMID 42094624), and a
systematic review found cognitive outcomes **mixed** (PMID 41917889). Send water;
do not promise a payoff.

**[STRONG] Caffeine: no.** Soós et al. (2021) concluded **no safe dose has been
described for children** and **no positive effects** on developing brain function.
PMID 34886115

**[STRONG] Sugar — the finding is the opposite of the folk belief.**
Wolraich, Wilson & White (1995), *JAMA*, meta-analysis of 23 studies: the 95%
confidence interval for **all 14** measured constructs **included zero**. Sugar
does not affect children's behaviour or cognitive performance. PMID 7474248

And Hoover & Milich (1994): **all children received placebo**; mothers *told*
their child had eaten sugar rated them significantly **more hyperactive** and
became more controlling and critical. PMID 7963081

> Read that again. The effect was in the **parents**.

## 6. Praise

**[MODERATE-STRONG] Mueller & Dweck (1998), *JPSP*** — fifth graders praised for
**intelligence** rather than **effort** subsequently showed **less persistence
after setbacks**, less enjoyment, more ability-deficit attributions, and **worse
performance**. They also described intelligence as **fixed**. PMID 9686450

**[MODERATE-STRONG] Kamins & Dweck (1999), *Developmental Psychology*** — the
age-matched study, **children aged 5-6**: person-directed feedback **including
positive person feedback** fostered helpless responses, self-blame and contingent
self-worth more than process-focused feedback. PMID 10380873

**[CONTESTED] Growth-mindset *interventions*.** Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023),
*Psychological Bulletin*, 63 studies, N = 97,672: overall **d = 0.05**; among the
highest-quality studies, **d = 0.02**, indistinguishable from zero. PMID 36326645.
A more favourable synthesis (Burnette et al. 2023) finds d = 0.14 for targeted
subsamples with high fidelity, but with a 95% prediction interval of -0.08 to 0.35.

> **The defensible position:** evidence that *packaged growth-mindset programs
> raise achievement* is weak and disputed. Evidence that *how you word praise
> changes a child's response to setbacks* is older, more direct, and better
> matched to this age group. Recommend the phrasing. Do not sell a workshop.

## 7. Talking to the child about the test

**Davidson Institute** practical guidance: frame it as helping teachers
understand how the child learns; explain it differs from a classroom quiz; tell
the child there will be questions they cannot answer because the test spans many
ages; **do not share the actual score with the child**, because children read
numbers as fixed definitions of their ability.
<https://www.davidsongifted.org/gifted-blog/how-to-explain-iq-testing-to-your-gifted-child/>

## Six claims this guide deliberately does NOT make

1. ~~"Write down your worries before the test."~~ The source study failed a
   preregistered replication, and was tested on teenagers and adults.
2. ~~"Power posing boosts performance."~~ Does not replicate.
3. ~~"A high-protein low-GI breakfast improves test scores."~~ Composition
   effects cannot be firmly concluded.
4. ~~"Sugar will make your child hyper and hurt the test."~~ The *JAMA*
   meta-analysis found no effect; the documented effect was on parents.
5. ~~"The sleep two nights before matters more than the night before."~~ Could
   not be verified.
6. ~~"X% of elementary children have test anxiety."~~ No defensible figure exists
   for grades 1-4.
