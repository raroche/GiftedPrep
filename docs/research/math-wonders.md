# Big Ideas — picking topics that make a child love mathematics

> **Scope note.** Florida's B.E.S.T. benchmarks are used here as one
> concrete, published set of US grade-level expectations to build and check
> against. The site itself is not state specific; grade-level maths
> expectations are close enough across US states for this to hold.

The first eight grade 1 topics are school mathematics done deeper. They are
useful and they are aligned to Florida's benchmarks, but no child has ever
fallen in love with place value. This document is the research behind a second
set of topics with a different job: show a six-year-old what mathematics
actually is, and let them do some.

## The bar these topics have to clear

Four tests. A topic only made the list if it passes all four.

1. **Real mathematics.** Not enrichment worksheets. Something a working
   mathematician would recognise as their subject.
2. **A wow moment.** A point where the child's face changes. Usually a result
   that is surprising, or a rule they discover themselves and can then use to
   predict.
3. **Touchable at six.** No reading load, no arithmetic prerequisite beyond
   counting to twenty. Ideally doable with hands, paper and scissors.
4. **A real-world hook.** Somewhere the idea actually lives: bees, snowflakes,
   maps, socks, a postman's route.

## The evidence that this works at this age

This is the part that decided the list. These topics are not a guess about what
young children might handle. They are documented accounts of what young
children *did* handle.

**Alexander Zvonkin, *Math from Three to Seven*.** A research mathematician ran
a maths circle in his Moscow apartment for his son and neighbours, starting at
age three or four. The book is his session-by-session journal. The actual
session titles include:

> The Möbius band · What is bigger, the whole or its part? · The Tower of Hanoi
> · A bit of topology · Intersecting classes · We start probability theory ·
> Snowflakes · Magic squares · Generalized chains · Isomorphic problems ·
> True and false statements · Breaking a code · A genealogical tree ·
> Homeomorphism · Four colors

That is a list of genuine mathematics — topology, combinatorics, probability,
graph theory — taught to preschoolers, by someone who wrote down what worked
and what failed. Session 29 is titled "Total failure" and session 38 "Losing my
grip", which is why the book is trustworthy.

**Joel David Hamkins, "Math for seven-year-olds".** A logician taught a class of
seven-year-olds graph colouring, chromatic numbers, map colouring, the four
colour theorem, Eulerian paths and circuits, and the Seven Bridges of
Königsberg. The children made their own graphs to challenge each other, drew
their own maps to colour, and worked out for themselves which figures could be
drawn without lifting the pencil. His account ends with a girl telling him she
wanted to be a mathematician.

Seven-year-olds. Graph theory. That single account is worth more than any
amount of theorising about developmental readiness.

**Julia Robinson Mathematics Festival** and **MathPickle** both build their
elementary programmes on low-floor high-ceiling puzzles rather than worksheets,
and both list a four-colour activity among their most popular. MathPickle's
stated aim is to put "hard, fun problems" into classrooms; its material is
organised by grade, and grade 1 gets genuinely hard problems, not easy ones.

## The eight topics, and why each one earns its place

### 1. Four Colours 🎨

Colour a map so no two countries that touch share a colour. Try to do it with
three. Discover you sometimes cannot. Then discover four is always enough, for
every map anyone has ever drawn.

**Why it is beautiful.** The statement is short enough for a six-year-old and
the proof took mathematicians 124 years and the first computer-assisted proof
in history. A child can fully understand the question and fully appreciate that
nobody could answer it for over a century.

**Real world.** Mapmakers. Also mobile phone towers, exam timetables and
seating plans, which are the same problem wearing different clothes.

**Proven at this age.** Hamkins, with seven-year-olds. JRMF's "Four Color
Challenge".

### 2. One Line, No Lifting ✏️

Can you draw this shape without lifting your pencil and without going over any
line twice? Some you can, some you cannot. The rule for telling which is
countable by a six-year-old: **count the corners with an odd number of lines.
Zero or two means yes. Anything else means no.**

**Why it is beautiful.** The child discovers a rule, and then the rule lets them
*predict* before trying. That is the first time most people experience what a
theorem is for. It is also the birth of graph theory: Euler settled the
Königsberg bridges in 1736 and invented a branch of mathematics doing it.

**Real world.** A postman who wants to walk every street once. A snowplough. A
museum guard's route. The actual bridges of a real city.

**Proven at this age.** Hamkins, with seven-year-olds.

### 3. The Bees' Secret 🐝

Which shapes tile the floor with no gaps? Triangles yes, squares yes, hexagons
yes, pentagons no. Then: why did bees pick the hexagon out of the three that
work?

**Why it is beautiful.** The answer is that a hexagon holds the most honey for
the least wax, and it is a real optimisation result — the honeycomb conjecture,
proved by Thomas Hales in 1999. The bees got there first.

**Real world.** As real as it gets. A bee must eat about eight pounds of honey
to make one pound of wax, so the saving is survival, not elegance.

### 4. Snowflakes and Mirrors ❄️

Fold paper, cut, unfold. Count how many mirror lines a shape has. Discover that
a snowflake always has six.

**Why it is beautiful.** Symmetry is the idea that connects a butterfly, a
snowflake, a cathedral and the standard model of particle physics. Starting it
with scissors at six is not a watered-down version, it is the real thing.

**Real world.** Snowflakes, butterflies, faces, flowers, buildings, letters.

**Proven at this age.** Zvonkin, session 42.

### 5. The Twisted Loop 🌀

Make a paper loop with one half-twist. Draw a line down the middle without
lifting the pen; it comes back to where it started having covered *both* sides.
So it has one side. Then cut along the line and it does not fall into two
pieces.

**Why it is beautiful.** It is the single best wow moment in elementary
mathematics. Nothing is calculated. A child holds a physical object that
behaves impossibly, and that object is a genuine mathematical example that
working topologists still care about.

**Real world.** The recycling symbol is a Möbius band. So are some conveyor
belts, deliberately, so they wear evenly on "both" sides.

**Proven at this age.** Zvonkin, session 21, with children around five.

### 6. Socks in the Dark 🧦

The drawer has black socks and white socks, mixed up, and the light is broken.
How many must you grab to be certain of a matching pair?

The answer is three, and it does not matter whether there are ten socks or ten
thousand. That last part is the mathematics.

**Why it is beautiful.** It is the pigeonhole principle, a real proof technique
used throughout mathematics, and it produces *certainty* rather than a good
guess. It also teaches the difference between "probably" and "definitely",
which most adults never get straight.

**Real world.** Getting dressed in the dark. Also: in any group of thirteen
people, two share a birth month, guaranteed, with no information about anyone.

### 7. The Tower 🗼

Move a stack of discs to another peg. Only one at a time, never a bigger disc
onto a smaller one. Three discs takes 7 moves. Four takes 15. Five takes 31.

**Why it is beautiful.** The child finds the pattern themselves: double it and
add one. It is recursion, and it is the clean case where solving a smaller
version of a problem solves the bigger one. The legend attached to it — monks
moving 64 discs, and the world ending when they finish — is a lesson in how
fast doubling runs away, since 64 discs takes longer than the age of the
universe.

**Real world.** Doubling. Folding paper, rice on a chessboard, and why a small
daily saving becomes large.

**Proven at this age.** Zvonkin, session 23.

### 8. Handshakes 🤝

Everyone at a party shakes hands once with everyone else. Six people, how many
handshakes? Then: draw six dots and join every pair. Same numbers. Then: 1, 3,
6, 10, 15 — the triangle numbers, already met in the patterns topic.

**Why it is beautiful.** Three problems that look nothing alike turn out to be
the same problem. Zvonkin spent several sessions on exactly this, giving the
children combinatorial problems that were all secretly identical and waiting
for them to notice. Recognising that two problems are the same is close to a
definition of mathematical maturity, and children can start it early.

**Real world.** Parties, tournament fixtures, and how many cables you need to
connect a room full of computers.

**Proven at this age.** Zvonkin, sessions 45 to 47, "Generalized chains",
"Isomorphic problems", and the story of the problem he calls "52".

## What got considered and cut

**Magic squares** (Zvonkin session 44). Cut from grade 1: with shaky arithmetic
it is practice wearing a hat. **Now the grade 3 topic**, where fluent times
tables turn it into a real deduction puzzle with exactly one answer.

**Probability** (Zvonkin sessions 28, 31, 36). Genuinely wonderful, and Zvonkin
found it hard going. Cut from grade 1 because it cannot be honest without
fractions. **Now the grade 3 topic "Chance"**, where fractions make it possible
to say how likely rather than just which is likelier.

**Infinity / whole versus part** (Zvonkin session 22). The most beautiful item
on his list. Cut from grade 1 because the honest version needs the child to be
comfortable with the counting numbers going on forever, and grade 1 is still
building the first hundred. **Now the grade 3 topic "Forever".**

**Binary and codes** (Zvonkin session 52). **Now the grade 2 topic "Secret
Codes"**, reached through doubling rather than base arithmetic.

**Knots and homeomorphism** (Zvonkin sessions 65, 66). Needs string and a pair
of hands more than a screen.

## How these are taught differently from the first eight

The first eight topics teach a skill and then practise it. These do not have a
skill to practise. So the shape changes:

- **Discover, then use.** The lesson poses the question before it gives the
  rule. In One Line, No Lifting, the child tries several shapes, is shown the
  odd-corner rule, and then has to *predict* the next one before drawing it.
- **Scissors and paper are the point, not a fallback.** The Möbius band, the
  snowflake and the tower are all better with hands. Those exercises say so and
  ask the child to come back with what happened.
- **An honest hard one at the end.** Each topic finishes with something a first
  grader may not get. That is deliberate: a child who is never stuck never
  learns that being stuck is normal, and gifted children in particular tend to
  meet that far too late.
- **The history stays in.** Königsberg was a real city. Euler was a real person.
  Four colours went unproved for 124 years. Children find it much easier to care
  about a problem somebody once could not solve.
