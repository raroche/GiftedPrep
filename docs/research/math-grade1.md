# Advanced Grade 1 Math — what to teach and why

> **Scope note.** Florida's B.E.S.T. benchmarks are used here as one
> concrete, published set of US grade-level expectations to build and check
> against. The site itself is not state specific; grade-level maths
> expectations are close enough across US states for this to hold.

This is the research behind the Grade 1 Math Lab page. It answers two
questions: what does a first grader already have to learn in Florida, and what
does "advanced" mean on top of that without simply handing a six-year-old
second grade worksheets.

## What the research says "advanced" should mean

The literature splits enrichment (going deeper inside the grade) from
acceleration (going faster into the next grade). The Study of Mathematically
Precocious Youth at Johns Hopkins has decades of evidence supporting subject
acceleration, but the same body of work warns that acceleration is abused when
a child is simply pushed through textbooks faster, which prevents any depth.
The consistent recommendation is a blend: mostly depth, with acceleration where
the child is genuinely ready.

That shapes this page. Roughly three quarters of it is depth on grade 1 ideas,
and about a quarter reaches into grade 2 and 3 territory where a bright first
grader can go without gaps.

Two further design ideas from the research:

**Low threshold, high ceiling.** NRICH's phrase for a task everyone can start
and nobody finishes quickly. Every topic here ends with an open challenge that
has more than one answer, so a child who finds it easy has somewhere to go.

**The equals sign is the big one.** A large majority of primary children read
`=` as "now write the answer" rather than "is the same as". They will accept
`4 + 5 = 9` but reject `4 + 5 = 6 + 3` as wrong. This misconception is
well-documented and predicts later trouble with algebra. Fixing it at six costs
nothing and is one of the highest-value things on this page.

## The eight topics, and where each one comes from

| # | Topic | Grade 1 root | Where it reaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ten Frames | NSO.2.1 | Subitizing: reading a quantity without counting |
| 2 | Number Bonds | NSO.1.3, AR.2.1 | Part-whole structure, fact families |
| 3 | Doubles and Odd or Even | NSO.2.1 | Parity, a grade 2 idea (MA.2.AR.3.1) |
| 4 | The Balance | AR.2.2 | Equivalence, the root of algebra |
| 5 | Missing Numbers | AR.2.3 | Unknowns in any position |
| 6 | Tens and Ones | NSO.1.2, NSO.2.3 | Place value past 100 |
| 7 | Equal Groups | NSO.1.1 skip counting | Arrays and early multiplication, grade 2–3 |
| 8 | Patterns and Shapes | GR.1.3, FR.1.1 | Growing patterns, polyominoes, halves and fourths |

The topic spine mirrors the Art of Problem Solving Beast Academy level 1
sequence, which is the best known published advanced curriculum for this age:
counting and shapes, then addition and subtraction, then comparison and
patterns, then big numbers and problem solving. Their level 1 explicitly
includes subitizing, ten frames, hundreds charts, polyominoes and
rotations, which is why those appear here rather than more worksheet drill.

**The parity link is the nicest single idea on the page.** An even number is
exactly a doubles fact, and an odd number is a doubles fact plus one. That
single sentence connects topic 3 to topic 1 and gives a six-year-old a real
theorem rather than a rule about last digits. Teaching odd and even as "ends in
0, 2, 4, 6, 8" is faster but teaches nothing; teaching it as "can everyone find
a partner" is the mathematics.

## How the exercises are designed

Not reading heavy. Prompts are one short line. The picture carries the
question, using ten frames, number bonds, arrays and balances rather than
paragraphs.

Six exercise types, so a session never feels like a worksheet:

| Type | What the child does |
|---|---|
| `choice` | Taps one of a few pictures or numbers |
| `number` | Types a number, checked as they go |
| `truefalse` | Decides whether an equation is true, feeding topic 4 |
| `build` | Fills a ten frame or an array by tapping, then checks it |
| `paper` | Leaves the screen, works it out with a pencil, comes back and enters the answer |
| `collect` | Open-ended: finds as many answers as they can, and the page keeps score |

`paper` exists because handwriting the work is where arithmetic actually
sticks, and because a screen that never asks a child to leave it is training
the wrong habit. `collect` is the low-threshold-high-ceiling slot.

Every exercise carries a short explanation shown after the answer, in the same
voice as the rest of the site: what the answer is, and why the tempting wrong
one is wrong.
